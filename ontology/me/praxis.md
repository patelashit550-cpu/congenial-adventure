## The Architecture of Social Network

*Acts I and II were a retrospective of my life so far — detailing my beginnings and career while tracing the fundamental tensions we all face as we express ourselves within that singular constraint described by the social sciences: the tension between self-sovereignty and collectivism.*

*Act III sets out what I came away with — a worldview that informs and guides my life as I live it today. It concerns our milieu: the social and intellectual ferment of humanity, something we have come to call the Social Network, as it reconciles the individual's desire for self-actualization with our collective need for security, while confronting the existential threats bearing down on each: climate change, global conflict, and the deepening inequality that drives both.*

*Throughout I have tried to introduce myself to you in a way that simultaneously sets up a comprehensive map of meaning— an ontology of the Social Network that provides one lens through which we may view our most pressing problems.*

It's relatively easy to reach middle age with your idealism intact from within the comfortable core of the prosperous West — where we are all possessed with the means required for personal self-improvement and elevation in every *notional* sense. Where only persistence, intention and courage are lacking for those who those say the cannot.

I have had the luxury of absorbing and applying ideas that matter — ones many would consider obscurely academic and impractical: Western philosophical traditions of the stoics for example and of Christ-consciousness;  but as well as their Eastern equivalents कर्म (karma) and 気 (ki); traditions which converge on the same essential discipline of *praxis*. A practice that entails learning and generally allowing those teachings to have a telling impact onself.

They are important today because our networks are developing a form of consciousness and need universal standards and mechanisms to guide them as protocols which may consistently and autonomously govern our interactions safely.

The balance between orthodoxy and fluid practice is never settled — only negotiated piecemeal in the rough of reality, producing something deeply personal. Theory provides the mental model; reality provides the terrain that enriches it. You march with others where the path feels true *for you* and improvise alone when it doesn't, within the constraints law imposes collectively. The bureaucracies and institutions we inherit struggle to contain the underlying mayhem — human nature pressing against those constraints at every seam.

Yet these institutions, and the freedoms they protect, remain the only instruments available for building a genuinely better future. Like praxis itself, they must evolve. It is the gaps as much as the stones that define a path; the care with which they are brought together determines whether it holds.

My own career required mastering the theory and nomenclature of project management and acquiring its widely respected accreditations — the PMP, CSM, ACP — each requiring demonstrated experience in the profession. I accumulated them while deployed across virtually every large institution in Toronto: with IBM, then as an independent contractor, then with Thomson Reuters, from 1999 to 2022.

I did this to leave a *mark* — on the social network itself and on those participants I found worth impressing. We are social animals. We cohere through the people and institutions we admire or at least respect — and through them become meaningful parts of the whole rather than appendices to it, redundant and without utility. My work was always one vehicle for something larger: how we *interface* with one another across the architecture of the *social* network — in open, honest dialogue, solving real problems, balancing individual sovereignty with communitarian utility, and deploying our accumulated capital, intellectual and otherwise, toward that end.

Above all we need to arrest an unfolding tragedy: our collective criminal negligence toward the global commons — the climate, the environment, the planetary inheritance we are actively squandering. The endless extraction might be forgivable if it had purchased satisfaction. It hasn't. I cannot work at the nexus of governance and information technology — efficiently, sustainably, or in good conscience — while pretending otherwise.

We do all of this quietly, within the norms of civil society, honouring the reasonable expectations others hold for us: that we show up, contribute, and fulfil our potential in the small ways that accumulate to mean something. We are each here to wrestle with ideas and put them to use.



### The Arc: Project, Product, Protocol

Project management entails the governing of risks that threaten a project's intended outcomes. For a long time, the project was the primary mechanism through which large institutions evolved and adapted to changing realities — in application development, that meant keeping apps relevant as utilities, or *nodes*, in people's lives: useful, engaging, even *delightful*.

The institutions I served were large and complex. Even their information technology units comprised myriad departments, each working interdependently with several others to deliver their application; this was a cumbersome environment demanding patience and persistence above all else. Project grinded slowly in fits and starts. They consisted of many pieces painstakingly lined up based on dependencies or pre-requisites in a chain of causality. The odds of a project succeeding perfectly were always vanishingly small. When projects were successfully concluded they were often delivered late, short of what was promised and at a considerably higher cost.

One had to be content to with the steady accumulation of knowledge and its registration into a general-purpose framework for understanding reality Something one carried to the next endeavour and which more importantly furnished the diligent and attentive project manager with a comprehensive worldview. This, in my view, is why we work at all, in any profession; it is why we live; to explore and experience life's rich pageant and grapple with how we as individuals fit into the larger picture of communities, institutions, sovereign collectives, and the global commons.

The project transformed states within a finite time period. It had a definite start and a definite end, both anchored by significant ceremonies whose purpose was to define two states — the current and the desired — and establish a plan to navigate between them.

The product has since superseded the project. A product amounts to a project with no discernible end: following an initial launch, it faces the marketplace as a continuous offering, persisting so long as it resonates. Resonance transfers value continuously between user and application in exchange for an investment of attention and energy. Governance has adapted to keep teams focused on the customer — adopting the improvisational frameworks of Agile and DevOps to identify opportunities and manage risks, teams now govern themselves with considerable leeway inside an overarching firm-level hierarchy. They answer to one thing alone: a relatively attractive return on investment. Yield.

Whether the unit of analysis is the project or the product, the primary threat is *waste* — attention and energy spent badly, generating discontent and dissatisfaction for both parties. Traditionally, teams contained this risk through the triple constraint: money, time, and scope. Today the distinction between value and waste is a fine line users draw based on how, why, and where they engage. The larger the scope, the more complexity compounds — which is why Agile and DevOps emerged, enabling small, self-governing teams to work with relative autonomy. In my experience, yield correlates directly to something simpler: fun, enjoyment, and contentment.

Software development's pervasive social impact is forcing a reckoning with how the field governs itself — and that governance shares a common underlying problem with the self-governance of teams: identity. The abstraction of identity — representing yourself digitally without compromising personal safety — sits at the centre of this transition. In a well-governed liberal pluralist society, this means personal privacy: ownership of your own data, protected from appropriation and use in ways that compromise your security, whether notional or material.

Each of us engages with the network in ways that aggregate into a precise signature — and in a world of finite resources, that signature carries weight. Protocols signal our relative preferences at a granular level and register the degree to which those preferences shape society. As such they express a measure of social credit: how much trust others may safely extend to us within a particular semantic perimeter. These semantic perimeters nest within a single overarching claim — the planet as a whole — which needs to be sustained as a collective inheritance and delivered to posterity in better condition than it was received.

The evolution from project to product to protocol is the long arc of human history compressed and relayed through the last one hundred or so years as history of information technology. From rigid hierarchies and their grand projects to the continuous loops creating value to rapidly assert dominance to ultimate demand of a network in need of transitioning to a sustainable equitable future of decentralized value-registering protocols

At the level of the transaction, how money is earned and spent sends a signal to the entire network about the choices a person makes over time—revealing basic values as much as personal attributes and life outcomes. In any jurisdiction, those outcomes tend to hinge on connections, access to capital, and one's relative value to a regime, rather than on intelligence, experience, ability, or any notion of personal virtue. The network is fueled by the endless consumerism it requires, punishing even those who have not fully renounced it but merely possess a self-styled, quiet detachment. There is also the banal poverty of the marginalized, who are excluded from incumbent financial systems and reduced to using paper cash. These are the homeless, the unemployed, and those who, for whatever reason, have been deemed untrustworthy—socially sanctioned and rendered invisible to the network.

This is no longer merely a social concern. For the creative among us, building an online presence to establish self-evident credibility has become a political imperative. ## Political Economy

In the liberal west three sets of interests form the *intellectual* foundation of both the liberal school of political economy which is beginning to supplant the realist school as a way to model how an actor expresses power. Fungible power (or coercive force) is becoming untenable even by large states because of its cost in reputation and brand terms while non-fungible power (influence) grows as mechanism to effect change by the individual. Traditionally the actors wielding non-fungible power were:

- **Firms** through the ethical-framing of the idea focusing on revenue above all else
- **Managers** who are typically not themselves alone material owners of a firm in the broad economy but beholden to passive shareholders
- **Governments** who intervene with fiscal and monetary policy to maintain societal order within their domains

The Austrian School of Economics has luminaries such as Friedrich Hayek and Ludwig von Mises who believed that any kind of central planning should become unnecessary in a pluralist domain, perimeter or jurisdiction in which local knowledge (beginning with an *own* individual's personal choices and preferences) dictate outcomes based on reason and sober intuition. Friedrich Hayek’s most famous concept is *Spontaneous Order*—the optimistic idea that complex systems (like language or markets) are generative and that an anarchic governance emerges from seemingly atomistic individual actions occurring without central planning. The behavioural school of economics focuses on the psychology and the influence applied to manipulate individual decision-making by an individual decisions including financial ones. These serve as a foundation for understanding the political economy supplanting the classical school which is now reduced to the heuristic that in any given market demand for a product (digital or otherwise) will be met with a supply for it and the choices made by individuals within the constraints imposed by law and social norms. In a pluralist liberal society individual choices will be expressed within a wide range of what is considered normal in society. However it is important to remember that data traverses national boundaries (with their various models for governance) with ease. Information is money ultimately and the most important - the identity as a source from which data emerges will be seen as the unit of measure and the metric used to assess values and trustworthiness in a universally coordinated manner where public reputation becomes either profoundly relevant for an individual or meaningless as a way to measure utility an individual's sense of worth.

Semantic perimeters (communities) grounded in alignment and universal values are now emerging in our networks as ideas are exchanged and gain currency as a trenchant foundation for living, creating, or merely being. However incumbent frameworks remain influential as earnings to pay for their personal living expenses (a salaried income) remain the objective of work for most people regardless of whether they find the work meaningful.



### 1. Firms

The first of those three ideas comes from the economist Milton Friedman who supplied a *moral* justification for maximizing profit. In 1970 Friedman wrote an article for The New York Times Magazine entitled “The Social Responsibility of Business Is to Increase Its Profits”. Its purpose was to *entrench* the limited liability corporation as an engine for innovation while the marketplace delineated which ideas were worth pursuing as products. As for the owners of these firms (increasingly passive shareholders) it was their right to spend their money as they wished; they were after all in the aggregate *consumers* of the same products they were creating.

His success in manufacturing a broad consent for this idea is evidenced by the focus on short-term results or quarterly earnings and a general satisfaction and appreciation for the consumer economy created. However the focus on money versus the product or service for example led to a subtle shift in emphasis; from the product itself to the *earnings* they produced. The idea is expressed by a quote attributed to Thomas Murphy (1915–2006), GM's Chairman and CEO during the 1970s:

> General Motors is not in the business of making cars. General Motors is in the business of making money.” In the tension between earnings and product it generally has been earnings that have won.   

Initially mass production was an innovation because it scaled production *without* compromising quality; not because it enabled profits per se but because it made a well-made product more affordable to a larger number. The process by which this became a reality was the assembly line and mass production of product at scale. Implied was the idea that manufacturing requires trade-offs between the necessary and the unnecessary in the product architecture involving how the qualities of a product and its price are optimised. Ford drew a hard line and since everything looked good in minimalist black that was the *only* colour his cars were made available in so capital may be focused elsewhere such as research and development.



### 2. Managers

In order to scale without incurring risk to capital without incurring additional debt the ownership of the firm was diluted to include thousands of ordinary people who given the limited liability of the corporation had no stake in the value (quality) of the product and were only interested in owning a share because of its prospects for generating wealth. So now the firm's success, and indeed even its very ability to operate as a going concern became dependent on its share price

The *mechanism* for implementing this tenet was explained by Jensen and Meckling in their 1976 paper — *Theory of the Firm*. When ownership (of the founder) separates from the day-to-day management of a firm, a principal-agent problem is created; namely how do owners led by so-called shareholder activists whose primary concern is profit attract, incentivize, and retain reliable loyal administrators focused on stock performance? Compensation became the lever to achieve those higher stock prices.

The shift in emphasis towards revenue has consequences. A firm's revenue in a given period are finite and an ever greater portion of the firm's income is devoted to paying its senior board-facing coterie. Internal income inequality within the corporation is best illustrated by a deteriorating Gini coefficient - a measure wherein a 1:1 ratio implies perfect equality with all employees earning the same salary: in 1965, a principal executive earned roughly 21 times the average wage but by 2024, that ratio had surged to 285:1. The extreme outlier in this trend is Starbucks, where the CEO’s compensation reached a staggering 6,666 times the pay of the average employee.

However, the window for true creative agency at large corporations is narrowing as AI is deployed to optimize, and eventually automate increasingly complex workflows. Work at the most sophisticated firms with the best talent empowers small self-governed teams working on products with Scrum and Kanban methods, affording them considerable autonomy.

These trends have led to some soul searching by workers whose skills traverse process engineering and other technical realms as well as content creation rooted in the liberal arts. They are not particularly motivated by higher salaries if it leads to increasingly administrative and political responsibilities far from solving real world problems for customers and instead require the need to compromise integral strength meeting arbitrary deadlines with long hours incurring both personal and professional technical debt - trauma inflicted as well to the network's social architecture. 

That social architecture is the sovereign's domain and in a world in which vast amounts of capital are transferred across national borders and yet still within a corporation's domain this sets up the profoundly difficult work of sorting legitimate from illegitimate behaviour. Namely behaviour that is both legal and ethical. Ultimately the decision to whether work is meaningful however is entirely subjective and the domain of the individual performing it.

### 3. Government

The shift towards knowledge work has changed the way in which the scope of government - regulatory affairs for example concerning health and safety (security of the person) are managed. An increasing proportion of our capital is digital rather than physical. Apart from time spent in the appreciation of aesthetic work (art or design) our attention to things correlate to the value or utility it has. Attention is now the generator of value (increasingly seen as the primary factor driving productivity). Content and access to it by humans versus bots seeking to scan for intelligence and to extract value (but not necessarily apply its logic) will be the ultimate source of logic governing both the network and one's agency as a participant on it and as well determine *social* credit. Digital content may be defined as anything which modulate attention to shape and frame the logic of consumer (which may now be a human, machine, or even just an object. To what end is now the primary objective of governance. If maximal extraction of value is the logic for a credible network then the question for a node (ultimately an end user) is of what value is that extraction to them, their community (the Sovereign) and the global commons for proof of stake communities and how should it be prioritized in a *finite* world? This is the tragedy of the commons all actors ultimately have a stake in solving regardless of ideology, philosophy or other factors motivating *human* behavior.

Whether at work or not we all spend an enormous amount of our time viewing, manipulating and creating digital value. On the internet now as persons with identity we all use the network, we are each visible to the network (and to varying degrees to each other) as an expression of how data is transformed and turned into value. Over time our collective attention aggregates to create a system of optimal paths develops between points on a network (users) to facilitate conservation of resources (time and energy). Over time the network generates a form of neural intelligence that operates in the interests of all its participants through the combination of content (memory) and the optimal pathways to them.
