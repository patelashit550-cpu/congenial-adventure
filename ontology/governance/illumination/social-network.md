## Political Economy

In the liberal west three sets of interests form the *intellectual* foundation of both the liberal and realist schools of political economy as a way to model how an actor expresses power. Fungible power (or coercive force) is becoming untenable even by large states because of its cost in reputation and brand terms while non-fungible power (influence) grows as mechanism to effect change by the individual. Traditionally the actors wielding non-fungible power were:

- **Firms** through the ethical-framing of the idea focusing on revenue above all else
- **Managers** who are typically not themselves alone material owners of a firm in the broad economy but beholden to passive shareholders
- **Governments** who intervene with fiscal and monetary policy to maintain societal order within their domains

The Austrian School of Economics has luminaries such as Friedrich Hayek and Ludwig von Mises who believed that any kind of central planning should become unnecessary in a pluralist domain, perimeter or jurisdiction in which local knowledge (beginning with an *own* individual's personal choices and preferences) dictate outcomes based on reason and sober intuition. Friedrich Hayek’s most famous concept is *Spontaneous Order*—the optimistic idea that complex systems (like language or markets) are generative and that an anarchic governance emerges from seemingly atomistic individual actions occurring without central planning. The behavioural school of economics focuses on the psychology and the influence applied to manipulate individual decision-making by an individual decisions including financial ones. These serve as a foundation for understanding the political economy supplanting the classical school which is now reduced to the heuristic that in any given market demand for a product (digital or otherwise) will be met with a supply for it and the choices made by individuals within the constraints imposed by law and social norms. In a pluralist liberal society individual choices will be expressed within a wide range of what is considered normal in society. However it is important to remember that data traverses national boundaries (with their various models for governance) with ease. Information is money ultimately and the most important - the identity as a source from which data emerges will be seen as the unit of measure and the metric used to assess values and trustworthiness in a universally coordinated manner where public reputation becomes either profoundly relevant for an individual or meaningless as a way to measure utility an individual's sense of worth.

Semantic perimeters (communities) grounded in alignment and universal values are now emerging in our networks as ideas are exchanged and gain currency as a trenchant foundation for living, creating, or merely being. However incumbent frameworks remain influential as earnings to pay for their personal living expenses (a salaried income) remain the objective of work for most people regardless of whether they find the work meaningful.



### 1. Firms

The first of those three ideas comes from the economist Milton Friedman who supplied a *moral* justification for maximizing profit. In 1970 Friedman wrote an article for The New York Times Magazine entitled “The Social Responsibility of Business Is to Increase Its Profits”. Its purpose was to *entrench* the limited liability corporation as an engine for innovation while the marketplace delineated which ideas were worth pursuing as products. As for the owners of these firms (increasingly passive shareholders) it was their right to spend their money as they wished; they were after all in the aggregate *consumers* of the same products they were creating.

His success in manufacturing a broad consent for this idea is evidenced by the focus on short-term results or quarterly earnings and a general satisfaction and appreciation for the consumer economy created. However the focus on money versus the product or service for example led to a subtle shift in emphasis; from the product itself to the *earnings* they produced. The idea is expressed by a quote attributed to Thomas Murphy (1915–2006), GM's Chairman and CEO during the 1970s:

> General Motors is not in the business of making cars. General Motors is in the business of making money.” In the tension between earnings and product it generally has been earnings that have won.   

Initially mass production was an innovation because it scaled production *without* compromising quality; not because it enabled profits per se but because it made a well-made product more affordable to a larger number. The process by which this became a reality was the assembly line and mass production of product at scale. Implied was the idea that manufacturing requires trade-offs between the necessary and the unnecessary in the product architecture involving how the qualities of a product and its price are optimised. Ford drew a hard line and since everything looked good in minimalist black that was the *only* colour his cars were made available in so capital may be focused elsewhere such as research and development.



### 2. Managers

In order to scale without incurring risk to capital without incurring additional debt the ownership of the firm was diluted to include thousands of ordinary people who given the limited liability of the corporation had no stake in the value (quality) of the product and were only interested in owning a share because of its prospects for generating wealth. So now the firm's success, and indeed even its very ability to operate as a going concern became dependent on its share price

The *mechanism* for implementing this tenet was explained by Jensen and Meckling in their 1976 paper — *Theory of the Firm*. When ownership (of the founder) separates from the day-to-day management of a firm, a principal-agent problem is created; namely how do owners led by so-called shareholder activists whose primary concern is profit attract, incentivize, and retain reliable loyal administrators focused on stock performance? Compensation became the lever to achieve those higher stock prices.

The shift in emphasis towards revenue has consequences. A firm's revenue in a given period are finite and an ever greater portion of the firm's income is devoted to paying its senior board-facing coterie. Internal income inequality within the corporation is best illustrated by a deteriorating Gini coefficient - a measure wherein a 1:1 ratio implies perfect equality with all employees earning the same salary: in 1965, a principal executive earned roughly 21 times the average wage but by 2024, that ratio had surged to 285:1. The extreme outlier in this trend is Starbucks, where the CEO’s compensation reached a staggering 6,666 times the pay of the average employee.

However, the window for true creative agency at large corporations is narrowing as AI is deployed to optimize, and eventually automate increasingly complex workflows. Work at the most sophisticated firms with the best talent empowers small self-governed teams working on products with Scrum and Kanban methods, affording them considerable autonomy.

These trends have led to some soul searching by workers whose skills traverse process engineering and other technical realms as well as content creation rooted in the liberal arts. They are not particularly motivated by higher salaries if it leads to increasingly administrative and political responsibilities far from solving real world problems for customers and instead require the need to compromise integral strength meeting arbitrary deadlines with long hours incurring both personal and professional technical debt - trauma inflicted as well to the network's social architecture.

### 3. Government

The shift towards knowledge work has changed the way in which the scope of government - regulatory affairs for example concerning health and safety (security of the person) are managed. An increasing proportion of our capital is digital rather than physical. Apart from time spent in the appreciation of aesthetic work (art or design) our attention to things correlate to the value or utility it has. Attention is now the generator of value (increasingly seen as the primary factor driving productivity). Content and access to it by humans versus bots seeking to scan for intelligence and to extract value (but not necessarily apply its logic) will be the ultimate source of logic governing both the network and one's agency as a participant on it and as well determine *social* credit. Digital content may be defined as anything which modulate attention to shape and frame the logic of consumer (which may now be a human, machine, or even just an object. To what end is now the primary objective of governance. If maximal extraction of value is the logic for a credible network then the question for a node (ultimately an end user) is of what value is that extraction to them, their community (the Sovereign) and the global commons for proof of stake communities and how should it be prioritized in a *finite* world? This is the tragedy of the commons all actors ultimately have a stake in solving regardless of ideology, philosophy or other factors motivating *human* behavior.

Whether at work or not we all spend an enormous amount of our time viewing, manipulating and creating digital value. On the internet now as persons with identity we all use the network, we are each visible to the network (and to varying degrees to each other) as an expression of how data is transformed and turned into value. Over time our collective attention aggregates to create a system of optimal paths develops between points on a network (users) to facilitate conservation of resources (time and energy). Over time the network generates a form of neural intelligence that operates in the interests of all its participants through the combination of content (memory) and the optimal pathways to them.
